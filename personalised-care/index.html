A new diagnosis brings a great deal of information at once. I created this website so that you can read about your condition at your own pace, at home and with your family. When we meet, we can then spend our time on your options and your choices.

You are in charge

When we first meet, you may notice that my approach to information and choice is different from what you are used to. I believe that you and I should make decisions about your care together.

Many patients, and many doctors, do not realise that the patient is the best person to be in charge of that decision. My background is in clinical trials and medical statistics, and I have reviewed the evidence extensively. Few decisions in clinical practice are clear cut, and many depend more on what matters to you than on medical fact.1

The General Medical Council asks doctors to explain the benefits and risks of any treatment or investigation, and to make decisions together with their patients. The British guideline on the radical management of lung cancer, written by a committee that I chaired and led, says the same.2

A treatment is recommended when its expected benefits outweigh its risks. I can tell you what those benefits and risks are. How much weight to give each of them is personal,3 that’s why only you can decide what is important to you.

You are in the driving seat of your care. I’ll provide the route map, and we’ll plan the journey together.

When it is hard to decide

Sometimes it is hard to decide, and patients ask, “What do you recommend?” or “What would you do if you were me?”

It is difficult for me to put myself in your shoes, because my values and yours may be quite different. So I ask about your values first. Together we work out what is and is not important to you, and that allows me to help you with the decision.

You do not have to decide on the day. Take the time you need, talk it over with your family, and bring someone with you to your consultation if that helps. If you would like a second opinion, I am happy to support that.

You tend to make the right decision

Making a decision can be difficult, and you may wonder afterwards whether you were right to accept or decline an operation. Patients tend to make the right decision for themselves, and that is more than a reassuring phrase.

We surveyed patients with poor lung function who came to my clinic, for whom surgery had the potential to make their lung function worse. About a year later, those who chose surgery and those who declined it were equally and highly satisfied with their decision.4

Your team

Personalised care is the work of a team, and you will come to know them. A clinical nurse specialist is there to support you and to answer your questions. My practice manager arranges your appointments and your admission. A research nurse can tell you about any studies you may be able to take part in.
